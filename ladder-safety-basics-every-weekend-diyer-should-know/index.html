Ladder Safety Basics Every Weekend DIYer Should Know

The core portable ladder safety rules from OSHA's official quick card, including the 3-point contact rule and the correct ladder setup angle.

Weekend DIYOct 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Falls from portable ladders, including step, straight, combination, and extension ladders, are described by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) as one of the leading causes of fatalities and injuries in workplaces. The same physics apply just as much in a garage or on a weekend painting job at home. OSHA's official Portable Ladder Safety QuickCard lays out the core rules in a short, practical list, and most weekend DIY ladder accidents trace back to skipping one of them.

Inspect before you climb

OSHA's first instruction is to read and follow all labels and markings on the ladder itself, since different ladder types and weight ratings have different rules printed directly on them. Before any use, inspect the ladder. If it's damaged in any way, OSHA's guidance is unambiguous: it must be removed from service and tagged until it's repaired or discarded, not used "just this once." A cracked rung or a bent rail does not get safer because the job is small.

OSHA also calls out electrical hazards specifically: look for overhead power lines before handling a ladder, and avoid using a metal ladder anywhere near power lines or exposed energized electrical equipment. This is easy to overlook when the job is something routine like cleaning gutters or hanging lights, precisely the kind of task where a ladder ends up near overhead wiring.

The 3-point contact rule

The single most repeated rule in OSHA's guidance is maintaining 3-point contact at all times while climbing: either two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand, in contact with the ladder at any given moment. Climb facing the ladder, with your body kept near the middle of the steps rather than reaching to one side. This rule exists because the moment you drop to 2-point contact, usually to reach for a tool or lean sideways to grab something, your margin for a slip or a loss of balance disappears.

OSHA's guidance also specifically prohibits standing on the top three rungs of a straight, single, or extension ladder, and prohibits using the top step or rung of any ladder as a step unless it was specifically designed for that purpose. Both of these common shortcuts remove your 3-point contact margin exactly when you're highest off the ground, which is the worst possible place for it to disappear.

Setup: angle, surface, and stability

OSHA specifies a precise setup angle for straight and extension ladders: the base should be placed a quarter of the ladder's working length away from the wall or vertical surface it's leaning against. A ladder set up too steep (base too close to the wall) is unstable and prone to tipping backward; one set up too shallow (base too far out) is prone to the feet sliding out from under it.

For extension or straight ladders used to access an elevated surface, OSHA requires the ladder to extend at least 3 feet above the point of support, giving you something stable to hold onto as you step off at the top.

The ladder also needs a stable, level surface to stand on, unless it has been secured at the top or bottom to prevent it from shifting. OSHA explicitly prohibits placing a ladder on boxes, barrels, or other unstable items to gain extra height, a shortcut that defeats the entire purpose of a properly sized ladder in the first place.

Don't move it, don't overload it, don't use it the wrong way

A few additional rules round out OSHA's guidance:

  • Never move or shift a ladder while a person or equipment is on it.
  • Only use ladders, and their accessories like levelers, jacks, or hooks, for their designed purpose.
  • Keep rungs, steps, and feet free of any slippery material.
  • Never use a self-supporting ladder, such as a step ladder, as a single ladder or in a partially closed position; it's only stable in its fully open, locked configuration.
  • Make sure all locks on an extension ladder are properly engaged before climbing.
  • Never exceed the ladder's maximum load rating, which includes the combined weight of you and any tools or materials you're carrying up with you.
  • If a ladder is set up somewhere it could be bumped by other activity, either secure it against displacement or put up a barricade to keep foot traffic away from it.

What NIOSH's injury data adds to the picture

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), part of the CDC, tracks why ladder falls happen rather than just how to avoid them. Its data attributes about 40% of ladder-related injuries to one specific cause: an incorrect extension ladder setup angle, which lines up directly with OSHA's quarter-length rule above. NIOSH's own recommendation is to set extension ladders at roughly a 75-degree angle, close enough to OSHA's setup rule that the two agencies' guidance reinforces the same practical setup check. NIOSH also notes that ladder falls are not just a workplace problem; many happen at home, and its data shows older and self-employed users face higher injury rates, which is exactly the population most weekend DIYers fall into.

Why this matters more for occasional DIY use than for professionals

Professional tradespeople use ladders daily and tend to internalize these rules through repetition. Weekend DIYers use a ladder a handful of times a year, often for the first time with a specific tool setup, which is exactly the situation where a rule gets skipped without anyone noticing until something goes wrong. Reading through this list once before a job, rather than assuming you remember the basics from last time, is a low-cost way to close that gap.

Key takeaways

  • Inspect a ladder before every use; a damaged ladder must be taken out of service entirely, not used carefully.
  • Maintain 3-point contact at all times while climbing, and never stand on the top three rungs or the top rung itself unless it's designed for that.
  • Set the base a quarter of the ladder's working length from the wall, and extend an extension or straight ladder at least 3 feet above the point of support.
  • Use a stable, level surface; never prop a ladder on boxes, barrels, or other unstable items for extra height.
  • Respect the ladder's maximum load rating, including the weight of any tools and materials you're carrying up.

The full OSHA Portable Ladder Safety QuickCard is one page, written for exactly this kind of quick pre-job check, and it's worth keeping bookmarked for the next time a project calls for climbing.
