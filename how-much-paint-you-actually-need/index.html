How Much Paint You Actually Need

A step-by-step room paint quantity calculation using manufacturer coverage specs, so you buy the right number of gallons the first time.

Weekend DIYOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Buying too little paint means a mid-project store run and a risk of color mismatch between cans from different batches. Buying too much wastes money on paint that sits in the garage. Both problems come from the same root cause: guessing at the math instead of calculating it.

The coverage number manufacturers actually publish

Paint coverage is typically listed per gallon, and it varies by brand, finish, and surface. Behr's product specification for its Cabinet, Door & Trim enamel states coverage "up to 400 sq. ft. per gallon," while Sherwin-Williams' own guidance states that "a gallon of paint typically covers about 350-400 square feet." Both manufacturers point to the same general range for smooth, primed surfaces, with the lower end of that range being the safer planning assumption, since real walls are rarely perfectly smooth and uniformly primed.

The formula, step by step

The full calculation has four steps:

  1. Gross wall area = 2 × (room length + room width) × ceiling height
  2. Paintable area = gross wall area - (doors × 20 sq ft) - (windows × 15 sq ft)
  3. Total area to cover = paintable area × number of coats
  4. Gallons needed = total area to cover ÷ coverage rate per gallon, rounded up

The 20 sq ft per door and 15 sq ft per window figures are standard estimation conventions for typical residential door and window sizes, used to avoid measuring every opening individually.

A worked example: a 12 x 14 foot bedroom

Room: 12 feet by 14 feet, 8-foot ceilings, 1 door, 2 windows, 2 coats, using the more conservative 350 sq ft per gallon coverage rate:

  1. Gross wall area: 2 × (12 + 14) × 8 = 416 sq ft
  2. Paintable area: 416 - (1 × 20) - (2 × 15) = 416 - 20 - 30 = 366 sq ft
  3. Total area to cover (2 coats): 366 × 2 = 732 sq ft
  4. Gallons needed: 732 ÷ 350 = 2.09, rounded up to 3 gallons

If you instead used the higher end of the manufacturer range (400 sq ft per gallon), the same room would calculate to 732 ÷ 400 = 1.83, rounding up to only 2 gallons. That one assumption, 350 versus 400 sq ft per gallon, is the difference between buying 2 or 3 gallons for the same room, which is why using the more conservative number is the safer default unless you know your specific wall surface is unusually smooth and well-primed.

A second example: a larger room

Room: 15 feet by 20 feet, 9-foot ceilings, 2 doors, 3 windows, 2 coats, at 350 sq ft per gallon:

  1. Gross wall area: 2 × (15 + 20) × 9 = 630 sq ft
  2. Paintable area: 630 - (2 × 20) - (3 × 15) = 630 - 40 - 45 = 545 sq ft
  3. Total area to cover (2 coats): 545 × 2 = 1,090 sq ft
  4. Gallons needed: 1,090 ÷ 350 = 3.11, rounded up to 4 gallons

Why rounding up matters more than it seems

Running short mid-project creates two separate problems. First, the obvious one: a delay while you go buy more. Second, a less obvious one: paint is mixed in batches, and even the same color code can have slight tint variation between batches (sometimes called a "dye lot" difference). A can bought weeks later to finish a wall can show a faint difference once both are dry, especially under strong light. Buying the full rounded-up amount in one trip avoids this risk entirely.

One case where you should stop and test first

If your home was built before 1978, the EPA's Renovate Right guidance explains that older paint layers underneath your current surface may contain lead, and disturbing those layers (sanding, scraping, or even some types of surface prep before repainting) can create lead dust hazards, particularly for children and pregnant occupants. This is a separate consideration from the paint quantity math above: if your project involves any sanding or scraping of older painted surfaces in a pre-1978 home, check the EPA's guidance on lead-safe work practices before starting, since the right precautions depend on the specific scope of surface disturbance involved.

Key takeaways

  • Manufacturer-published coverage for a gallon of interior paint is typically 350 to 400 sq ft on smooth, primed surfaces; using the lower end (350) is the safer planning assumption.
  • The full calculation is: gross wall area, minus door and window deductions (20 sq ft per door, 15 sq ft per window), times number of coats, divided by coverage rate per gallon, rounded up.
  • A 12x14 room with 8-foot ceilings needs about 3 gallons for 2 coats at 350 sq ft/gallon coverage, or 2 gallons at the more optimistic 400 sq ft/gallon figure.
  • Always round up to the next whole gallon and buy it all in one trip; paint batches can have slight color variation between purchases.
  • In homes built before 1978, check the EPA's Renovate Right guidance before any sanding or scraping of existing painted surfaces, due to potential lead paint hazards.

Measure your actual room, run the four-step formula above, and round up. It takes a few minutes and avoids both an awkward mid-project paint-store trip and a wall with two slightly different shades of "the same" color.
