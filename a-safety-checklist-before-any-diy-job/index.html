A Safety Checklist Before Any DIY Job

Official OSHA ladder safety guidance and EPA lead paint precautions, turned into a practical pre-project checklist for home DIY work.

Weekend DIYOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

Most home DIY injuries come from a small set of predictable situations: a ladder set up wrong, a tool used without the right protection, or old paint disturbed without knowing what is in it. None of these require specialized training to avoid, just a short checklist run through before starting.

Ladders: the single most common DIY hazard

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the federal agency responsible for workplace safety standards, publishes a Portable Ladder Safety QuickCard that applies just as well to home DIY work as it does to job sites. Key points from OSHA's guidance:

  • Read and follow all labels and markings on the ladder. Weight ratings and duty classifications are not generic; they are specific to that ladder model.
  • Avoid electrical hazards. Look for overhead power lines before positioning or carrying a ladder, and avoid using a metal ladder near electrical work of any kind.
  • Inspect before every use. A ladder that was fine last month can develop a crack, loose rung, or worn foot since the last time you used it.
  • Maintain three points of contact while climbing, meaning two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand, at all times.
  • Never stand on the top rung or step, and do not use the top step of a stepladder as a step.

A practical pre-climb routine: check the ladder's duty rating against your own weight plus tools, look up for any overhead power lines or obstructions, set it on firm and level ground, and confirm it extends enough past the working point that you are not stretching or leaning to reach your work.

Lead paint: a hazard specific to older homes

If your home was built before 1978, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Renovate Right guidance explains that lead-based paint may be present in layers underneath more recent paint. This matters specifically for any work that disturbs painted surfaces: sanding, scraping, demolition, or even some drilling and cutting through painted materials.

The EPA's guidance is aimed at protecting children and pregnant occupants in particular, since lead dust exposure carries developmental risks. Before starting any project in a pre-1978 home that will disturb existing paint:

  • Consider testing suspect painted surfaces for lead before sanding or scraping them.
  • If lead is present or suspected, look into lead-safe work practices (containment, wet methods to minimize dust, specific cleanup procedures) rather than standard sanding and cleanup.
  • This consideration applies regardless of how small the project seems; a single window frame being scraped can generate meaningful dust if lead paint is present underneath.

Personal protective equipment, matched to the actual task

A generic pair of work gloves does not cover every situation. Matching protection to the specific hazard matters:

  • Eye protection for any task producing dust, debris, or flying particles: sanding, cutting, drilling, or using a nail gun.
  • A properly rated dust mask or respirator for sanding, especially in older homes where lead paint is a possibility, or for any job producing fine particulate like drywall dust.
  • Hearing protection for sustained use of loud power tools, since hearing damage from tool noise accumulates with exposure time, not just peak volume.
  • Cut-resistant or work gloves suited to the specific tool; a glove appropriate for handling lumber is not necessarily appropriate for operating certain power tools, where loose gloves can actually be a hazard around moving parts.

Electrical and ventilation basics

Beyond ladders specifically, OSHA's broader hazard guidance for any home project includes checking for overhead or buried power lines before any task involving height or digging, and ensuring adequate ventilation when using products with strong fumes (solvents, certain paints, adhesives) in enclosed spaces.

A short pre-project checklist

Before starting, confirm:

  • Ladder duty rating checked against your weight plus tools, and the ladder inspected for damage
  • Overhead power lines checked if the ladder will be used outdoors or near utility lines
  • Home's construction date checked if sanding or scraping existing paint (pre-1978 means a lead paint consideration)
  • Eye, respiratory, and hearing protection matched to the specific task, not just "whatever is in the drawer"
  • Workspace ventilation confirmed adequate for any fumes involved
  • Three points of contact planned for any ladder work, with the top rung and top step off-limits

Key takeaways

  • OSHA's ladder safety guidance centers on checking the ladder's duty rating, inspecting it before every use, avoiding overhead power lines, and maintaining three points of contact while climbing.
  • Homes built before 1978 may have lead paint under newer layers; the EPA's Renovate Right guidance recommends testing and lead-safe work practices before sanding or scraping existing paint in these homes.
  • Personal protective equipment should match the specific task (dust, fumes, noise, flying debris), not a single generic glove-and-goggles assumption.
  • A short, repeatable pre-project checklist catches the same few hazards (ladder setup, lead paint, mismatched PPE, ventilation) that cause most home DIY injuries.
  • None of these precautions require special training; they require running through the checklist before starting rather than assuming "it will probably be fine."

Running this checklist takes a few minutes before any project. It is cheaper than a fall, a dust exposure, or a trip to urgent care.

Sources

  1. Occupational Safety and Health Administration, Portable Ladder Safety QuickCard
  2. U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Renovate Right
